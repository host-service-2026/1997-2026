Hazel Dooney was a fine artist, writer and strategist. She specialised in concept development, stakeholder communications, private project execution, literary essays, and combining written and visual communication to simultaneously speak to multiple audiences online. Throughout her career she demonstrated prescient asymmetric digital strategy, developed and managed strategic networks, and managed intellectual property.

Dooney’s early and mid-career paintings were a satirical reflection on the influence of advertising and popular culture, before both became interwoven with our lives via social media. These works were made with industrial materials and influenced by Tracey Moffatt, Cindy Sherman and Barbara Kruger. Her mature paintings were abstracts with subtle shifts in colour – executed in acrylic on linen and influenced by Agnes Martin and Mark Rothko – while her figurative 'pop' paintings evolved into nuanced contemporary portraiture, landscapes and still life, with the additional influences of Bridget Riley and Tamara de Lempicka.

From 1997 to 2001 Dooney produced major artworks for six solo exhibitions. She first received national attention in 1999, when one of her paintings was published in concurrent issues of _Vogue Australia_, _Belle_ and _Marie Claire_ magazines in response to a press release she sent independently.[1]

In 2001 Dooney was included in _The Weekend Australian_ with Jeffrey Smart and others in an article about artists who create work of a consistently high quality.[2] The same year, she was the youngest and only female artist in an expedition to central Australia with well-known established artists, including John Olsen. In 2002, this resulted in the coffee table book _William Creek & Beyond_ and documentary _The View from Here_, aired on ABC TV.[3][4][5]

In 2003 Dooney commissioned the website hazeldooney.com.[6] In 2004 she produced major artworks for a final solo exhibition as a represented artist. In 2005 she restructured her business to establish a direct-to-consumer model for fine art by connecting directly with the audience for her work via the internet, initially via email and a private newsletter disseminated via word of mouth.

In July 2006, during a one-off exhibition with progressive gallerist Andy Dinan, Dooney was featured on the front page of the _Business_ section of _The Age_ newspaper in an article titled 'State-of-the-art selling rivals play to the galleries'.[7] Of the art, critic Ashley Crawford wrote, "With her earlier work, one wondered whether she could in fact draw. _Venus in Hell_ removes all doubt."[8] Also in July, Dooney began her blog, 'Self vs Self'. In August her first essay, 'Life Study', was published by _Griffith Review_, with an extract published in _The Australian Financial Review_ in September.[9] Later the same month, a feature on Dooney was published in _The Courier Mail_’s magazine, _QWeekend_.[10] The tagline on the cover read, "Why painter Hazel Dooney is her own best agent." 

Dooney's primary business model was to create new series of artworks, and produce new works for each series via private commission, which continued throughout her career. She used online and email publishing as her marketing, alongside engagement with media until 2014. As the internet evolved to better host images, Dooney expanded her marketing to include documentary-style photography and short videos, which she filmed and edited herself.

In 2007 Dooney was included in _Vogue Australia_’s 'Australia’s Most Wanted', featuring cultural figures developing international success.[11] Two major paintings, _Sports Babe (Tennis)_ and _Sports Babe (Cricket)_, were sold by Christie's London in 2007 for 10,000 GBP each (23,000 AUD approx.). _Dangerous Career Babe: The Aviatrix_ was sold by Christie's London in 2008 for 14,375 GBP (32,200 AUD approx.).[12][13][14]

In 2011 Dooney was included as a case study in the 'Strategic Networks' chapter of university textbook _Strategy: Theory and Practice_.[15]

In 2013 her essay 'Broken' was published by _Griffith Review_ and included in _The Best Australian Essays 2013_, edited by Robert Manne. An edited extract, retitled 'Bad Education', was published in _Good Weekend_ magazine in _The Age_ and _The Sydney Morning Herald_ newspapers.[16] The related legal loophole that failed to protect Queensland school students aged 16 and 17 was closed 12 years later.[17]

Also in 2013, Lord Mayor Robert Doyle invited Dooney to create a public artwork for The City of Melbourne after reading her blog. The 15 metre text-based mural, _Ten Dicta for Young Women Who Are Artists_, was located on Royal Lane, off Bourke Street in Melbourne’s CBD, and remained beyond the initial agreement to 2019.[18]

From 2014 Dooney restructured to reduce her public profile, disengaging from interviews with media due to sensationalism and factually inaccurate 2013 profiles – including unverified claims regarding her medical history.[19] Instead, she expanded her direct-to-audience writing to increase asset scarcity and further tailored private stakeholder communications.

From 2018 to 2021 Dooney worked on an international private portrait commission. In response to the sitter, her figurative work became nuanced and incorporated landscape backgrounds. Until late 2022, she developed new abstract work exploring the way colour changes in natural light under the patronage of entrepreneur, investor and corporate advisor Mark Carnegie.

From 2022 to 2024 she wrote a column for _Coagula Art Journal_, founded and edited by American art critic Mat Gleason.[20]

In 2023 Dooney’s essay 'The future of art fraud' was published by _Griffith Review_ and she completed a major portrait of writer and art critic, Neha Kale.[21][22] From 2024 she worked on major private portrait commissions and developed the concept and digital strategy for _Everything the artworld doesn't want you to know_, a direct-subscription publication for Australia's leading art critic, John McDonald.[23] 

At the end of the 2026 financial year, Hazel Dooney formally concluded her career as an artist and essayist, deleted all social media accounts and finalised all business operations in alignment with Australian Taxation Office requirements.
